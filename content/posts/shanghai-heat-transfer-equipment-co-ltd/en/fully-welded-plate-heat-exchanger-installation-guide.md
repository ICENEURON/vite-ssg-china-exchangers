Fully welded plate heat exchangers are selected for duties where gasketed equipment may be limited by pressure, temperature, aggressive media, or leakage risk. Their performance depends not only on design and fabrication, but also on installation quality. Poor foundation work, pipe stress, rough lifting, or rushed commissioning can shorten service life before the exchanger has completed its first operating cycle.

## 1. Confirm Site and Foundation Conditions

Before equipment arrival, confirm that the installation area provides enough space for lifting, operation, inspection, and future maintenance. The foundation must carry the equipment weight, fluid weight, operating vibration, and connected piping loads. Its surface should be level, clean, and cured to the required strength.

Anchor bolt locations, base plate dimensions, nozzle orientation, and maintenance clearances should be checked against the latest approved drawings. Small layout errors can become expensive if discovered after the exchanger is on site.

## 2. Inspect the Equipment Before Lifting

After delivery, compare the packing list, nameplate, documents, and visible equipment condition. Check nozzles, lifting points, supports, weld areas, instruments, and protective covers. If damage is found, record it before installation begins.

Lifting should follow a reviewed lifting plan. Use rated slings and lifting points, avoid sudden movement, and protect welded surfaces from impact. Fully welded equipment may be robust in operation, but transportation and lifting damage can still affect alignment and sealing surfaces.

## 3. Control Piping Stress

Pipe connection is one of the most important installation steps. The exchanger should not be used as a pipe support or alignment tool. Forced alignment can transfer bending stress into nozzles and welded structures, increasing fatigue risk during thermal cycling.

Use proper pipe supports, guides, and expansion compensation. Hot and cold lines should be connected with attention to thermal growth, vibration, and maintenance access. Gaskets, bolts, and flange faces should be clean and correctly specified.

## 4. Install Instruments and Controls

Temperature, pressure, flow, and pressure-drop measurement points should be installed where operators can obtain meaningful data. For critical service, trend data is more valuable than occasional manual readings. Instruments should be calibrated before startup and wired according to the project control philosophy.

If the unit is connected to monitoring or automation systems, alarm limits should reflect design pressure, allowable pressure drop, temperature ramp rate, and process safety requirements.

## 5. Commission Gradually

Startup should be smooth. Avoid thermal shock by increasing temperature and flow gradually. Vent trapped gas, check drains, verify flow direction, and monitor pressure drop from the first circulation. Any abnormal vibration, noise, leakage, or rapid pressure change should be investigated before full-load operation.

After commissioning, record baseline data: inlet and outlet temperatures, flow rates, pressure drop, operating pressure, and duty. These values become the reference for later troubleshooting.

## Long-Term Operation

A fully welded plate heat exchanger can run reliably for years when temperature swings, pressure spikes, corrosive species, and solids are controlled. The installation team should therefore hand over more than a completed mechanical job; it should hand over a clean baseline, operating limits, and maintenance logic.

That discipline is what allows welded plate technology to deliver its main value: compact, high-integrity heat transfer under demanding process conditions.
