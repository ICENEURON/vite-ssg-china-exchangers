Heat exchangers rarely fail without warning. Most performance problems develop gradually through fouling, leakage, corrosion, flow imbalance, or poor operating discipline. A good maintenance program is therefore not only a repair activity; it is a continuous diagnostic system that protects energy efficiency, production uptime, and process safety.

## Common Failure Modes

Fouling is the most common cause of lost thermal performance. Scale, suspended solids, biological growth, oil films, or corrosion products create thermal resistance on the heat transfer surface. Operators may notice a smaller temperature approach, higher outlet temperatures, or rising energy use before a shutdown becomes necessary.

Leakage is more serious because it may cause cross-contamination between process streams. In gasketed plate heat exchangers, leakage can come from gasket aging, chemical attack, incorrect tightening, or plate deformation. In tubular equipment, tube-to-tubesheet joints and corrosion-thinned tubes are common risk points.

Corrosion reduces wall thickness and can create pitting or crevice attack. It is often linked to chloride concentration, pH drift, oxygen ingress, poor material selection, or cleaning chemicals that are too aggressive for the alloy.

Blockage restricts flow and raises pressure drop. In plants with fibers, crystals, debris, or suspended solids, blockage can also cause uneven flow distribution and local overheating.

## How to Diagnose Problems

Temperature and pressure data should be trended, not only checked during alarms. A rising pressure drop combined with falling heat duty often points to fouling or partial blockage. A sudden pressure change may suggest leakage, valve problems, or pump instability.

Visual inspection remains useful, especially around frames, gaskets, nozzles, welds, drains, and vent points. For internal issues, non-destructive testing such as ultrasonic thickness measurement, dye penetrant inspection, eddy current testing, or radiography may be required depending on equipment type.

Fluid analysis is another early warning tool. High hardness, high chloride, abnormal pH, metal ions, or biological activity can reveal why the exchanger is losing performance.

## Cleaning Strategies

Mechanical cleaning is direct and easy to understand. It includes brushing, scraping, flushing, or water jetting after opening the unit. It works well for removable plate heat exchangers and many tube bundles, but it must be controlled to avoid scratching heat transfer surfaces.

Chemical cleaning can dissolve scale and organic deposits without full disassembly. The chemical program should match the foulant and metallurgy. Acid cleaning can remove mineral scale, while alkaline solutions are often better for oils and organic residues. Concentration, temperature, exposure time, neutralization, and waste handling must all be managed.

Online cleaning is useful when downtime is costly, but it is not a universal substitute for offline inspection. It works best as a preventive measure before fouling becomes severe.

## Maintenance Program

A practical program combines routine data review, scheduled inspections, water or process-fluid treatment, replacement of worn components, and operator training. For critical heat exchangers, maintenance records should include pressure drop, duty, cleaning method, chemical concentration, replaced parts, inspection photos, and post-cleaning test results.

The goal is not simply to clean the exchanger. The goal is to understand why performance changed and prevent the same loss from returning too quickly.
